How to Design a Two-Name Ambigram for a Couple's Tattoo

This is the tattoo request we get asked about more than almost anything else: two names, one design, and it has to actually work when it's permanent. Here's the real process, not the simplified version.

Start With the Names, Not the Software

Before you open any design tool, write both names on paper, one above the other, and look honestly at whether they're a realistic match. A five-letter name and an eleven-letter name are going to fight you the entire way through — it's not impossible, but it usually means one name ends up visually dominant, which defeats the point.

The easiest pairs to work with are similar in length and share a few compatible letters. If your names genuinely don't pair well, it's worth knowing that upfront rather than discovering it after an hour of frustrated sketching.

How to Design a Two-Name Ambigram: Matching Letters Between Two Different Words

This is the part that makes two-name ambigrams meaningfully harder than a single-word rotational design: you're not looking for a letter that resembles itself upside down, you're looking for a letter in name A that can plausibly become a specific, different letter in name B.

Lay both names out so the last letter of name B lines up under the first letter of name A (since the design flips end-to-end), and go through position by position. Some pairs will click immediately — an S and an M share more curve structure than you'd expect. Others will need real distortion, and one or two positions might need a compromise you're not thrilled about. That's normal, even for professional letterers.

Sketch Before You Touch a Screen

Rough out both directions on paper first. You're solving a puzzle, and a pencil is faster for trial and error than any software's undo button. Once you have a rough version where every letter-pair at least plausibly works, move to Illustrator or Photoshop to clean it up using the same process as a single-word ambigram: build clean vectors, rotate and test constantly, and refine stroke weight until both names look intentional, not like one is a distorted afterthought.

Before You Book the Appointment

Print the design at the actual size and placement you're planning for the tattoo, and look at it under different lighting and from a slight distance — a lot of the fine detail that reads perfectly on a monitor gets lost at wrist or forearm scale. This is also the point to bring it to a tattoo artist for a technical review; they'll often catch spacing or line-weight issues specific to how skin ages that a screen never reveals.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do both names need to be the same length?

Not exactly the same, but close in length makes this dramatically easier. A big length mismatch usually means one name ends up visually smaller or simplified.

What if our names just don't pair well?

It happens. Sometimes a nickname, a middle name, or initials work better than full first names. It's better to find that out on paper than after the design is already inked.

Should I use a generator first?
